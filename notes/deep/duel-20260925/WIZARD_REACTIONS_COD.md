## Short reaction

The other scorers are directionally right about the center of gravity: `jev-kit`, an agent-first
contract, and a real omp consumer are better bets than another game benchmark. They are also right
to distrust active blocking, broad package claims, and a generic experiment framework that has no
reader.

Where I disagree: the experiment kernel is not merely “more testing.” The current measured defects
are precisely input/preflight failures that a shared kernel can refuse before spend, and the kernel
becomes product infrastructure once it drives the shadow consumer and stranger kit. Similarly,
shadow mode is not useless passive logging when the missing artifact is an outcome join; the join is
the first product feedback loop. I agree that shadow must have a promotion condition and cannot be
its permanent terminal state.

My most important correction is narrower than either other model’s strongest recommendation: the
native omp judge bridge is high leverage, but not yet an 885/zero-overhead product. The judge role,
key source, profile behavior, receipt path, and live model identity still need a small L2/L3 proof.
Conversely, the other scorers are too dismissive of game/planner work when it is a local, externally
graded mechanism demo; it is not the next product, but it is not automatically research theater.

## Detailed reaction to Claude / CC

### CC’s criticism of COD-1: shadow mode

Claude’s 870 is fair and correctly identifies the outcome join as the missing part. I disagree only
with the implication that shadow is weak because it is passive. In this lane, shadow is the safest
way to get organic labels and establish whether the seat transfers from the 78/100 replay. An active
hook before that label set exists would be a policy guess. My revised 910 is below my original 930
because the cost and browser/computer blind spots are material, not because shadow is a dead end.

### CC’s COD-2 score of 820

The reasoning is sound: TypeScript first, official SDK, and fewer files/failure modes. The score is
lower than GMI’s 940 for a good reason: publishing TS and Python surfaces, a CLI, and a kernel can
turn a thin client into a maintenance project. My revised 880 is a compromise. `jev-kit` wins only
if its first release is smaller than the current client plus duplicated demo plumbing.

### CC’s COD-3 score of 760

I accept the discount. The kernel is needed because today’s defects are preflight defects, not
merely statistical uncertainty, but a user does not buy a kernel. My revised 760 makes the condition
explicit: it is funded by the shadow consumer and kit, and it must migrate MiniWoB plus one real
consumer before new experiment features are added. The e-process is a kernel component, not a
separate milestone.

### CC’s COD-4 score of 640

This is too low on leverage but correct on evidence. Native `judge_batch` is exactly the kind of
reuse the repository’s own rules demand; the missing judge role/key/profile proof is an L2 blocker,
not a reason to abandon it. I revise from 850 to 790: high potential, but the first work is a
small profile/doctor/receipt smoke, not a claim that native omp is already a usable Jev surface.

### CC’s COD-5 score of 470

I agree with the caution about game/planner scope more than I did initially. The 24-task OSWorld
selector was stopped by arithmetic, and no planner demo is a product until it has a checker and a
real consumer. I do not accept a blanket “games are a distraction”: Jericho and local Pokémon have
real environment outcomes and can demonstrate a mechanism that ordinary CI cannot. Revised 570 for
`jev_select` and 480 for Jev-PUCT reflect “later, bounded, externally graded,” not “next.”

## Detailed reaction to Gemini / GMI

### Strong agreements

1. **GMI-11 / COD-2 at 940:** packaging the stable core is the right center, but 940 assumes the
   release problem is free. The current `work/jev-client` has no package manifest and the client’s
   measured seats are not interchangeable. My revised COD-2 is 880.
2. **GMI-4 / COD-13 at 640:** diff-review feedback needs real human outcomes; the existing seven
   hand-built labels and blunt real-commit proxy cannot certify it. Keep advisory and collect labels.
3. **GMI-3 / COD-12 at 380:** this is a useful correction to my own optimism. Existing compaction
   evidence failed its retention bar, so live dropping is not earned. My revised COD-12 is 620 and
   explicitly shadow-only until a new bar passes.
4. **GMI-17 / COD-16 at 850:** receipt-driven README generation addresses an actual maintenance
   failure. I retain 830 because generated results still need a reader and a live boundary.
5. **GMI-21 / COD-8 at 805:** taint-aware state construction is worth defense-in-depth, especially
   because `.omp` tools resolve keys in process memory and raw state is the sensitive payload. I
   retain 790, not 805, because schema friction can cause users to bypass the kit.
6. **GMI-23 / COD-15 at 590:** semantic caching deserves suspicion. An identical request can use a
   deterministic hash cache; paying Jev to decide whether to pay Jev needs a demonstrated hit rate
   and explicit expiry. My revised score is 650, with exact caching first.
7. **GMI-18 / COD-18 at 560:** the typed compiler is indeed over-abstracted. The wire contract is
   small, and generated policy code would make the core harder to understand. Revised 430.
8. **GMI-26--30 / COD-24--30:** the low scores on desktop/game tails are mostly right on execution
   risk. I only resist collapsing all games into “research theater”; local checkers and replay
   outcomes are legitimate external oracles. Their scores remain low after that correction.

### Where GMI is wrong or overconfident

#### Native Judge bridge (COD-4, GMI 885)

“Zero custom overhead” is not observed. The current profiles do not configure the judge role, the
key is not in the pane environment, and the project must prove model resolution, cost, and receipt
behavior. Native primitives lower implementation cost, but they do not provide an integration
contract for free. Revised 790, still one of the best ideas, with L2/L3 proof as the first task.

#### Compaction (COD-12, GMI 870)

The proposed retention invariants are sensible, but the score ignores the project’s actual failed
bars: prior compaction keep rules retained 0/32 needed results under one cut and did not clear the
promotion bar. “Preserve errors/diffs” is a hypothesis, not a proof. GMI’s desired 40–60% token
saving is unmeasured. The correct sequence is shadow labels -> held-out bar -> tiny action surface;
revised 620.

#### Cache gate (COD-15, GMI 590)

GMI is right about semantic invalidation, but it underweights exact cache reuse. A canonical hash of
(model, state, questions, policy version) can be a boring, deterministic latency/cost optimization
with no semantic judgment. The semantic validity gate remains low; the exact cache sub-idea raises
the combined score to 650, not 590.

#### Active safety (GMI-2’s criticism of COD-1)

GMI correctly rejects day-one active blocking. I disagree that shadow mode creates “no product
value”: it creates the real outcome join the current system lacks. The revised design is staged:
shadow always, review suggestion next, blocking only after a held-out false-alarm/recall bar. I
therefore retain COD-1 near the top at 910 and reject GMI’s implied shortcut from replay accuracy
to enforcement.

#### Game dismissal

GMI scores COD-26--29 in the 390--530 range and calls the category disconnected from developer
needs. The practical criticism is valid for the roadmap: games should not displace omp/kit work.
The categorical claim is too strong. A local game score/checker is often cleaner ground truth than
human review, and the planner/prior mechanism is where Jev’s distribution is technically distinctive.
I keep small revised scores (450--480) and move them after the product core.

## Final reaction

The cross-score changes my priorities in three concrete ways:

1. **Lower the native bridge from “nearly ready” to “high-potential, unproven” (COD-4: 850 ->
   790).** The first deliverable is an L2 profile/doctor/receipt smoke, not a live claim.
2. **Downgrade active/automatic compaction (COD-12: 700 -> 620).** The existing failures are a
   direct counterexample to GMI’s confidence; shadow labels and a real bar come first.
3. **Downgrade the trajectory selector (COD-21: 650 -> 570).** The underpowered 24-task stop was
   correct arithmetic, not a reason to lower the bar. A full-power universe is a future option,
   not current product evidence.

I keep the central architecture: shadow consumer -> thin kit -> preflight/receipts -> native omp
batch -> one powered action-taking selector. The other scorers correctly pushed against overbuilding,
but their strongest disagreement with COD is about sequencing, not mechanism. The observable next
move remains one organic omp decision with a label and one stranger path that runs in five minutes;
none of these reactions is a Jev model result.
